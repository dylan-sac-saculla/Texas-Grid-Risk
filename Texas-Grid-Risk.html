The Texas Grid Squeeze: when the weather pushes demand up and supply down at once

You run electricity planning for the Texas grid (ERCOT). On the hottest afternoons, air conditioning drives record demand. On the coldest mornings, electric heating drives demand up too, but the same cold can freeze gas wells, pipelines, and power plants. Your job is to keep supply above demand on both kinds of days. What's the problem?

Electricity has to be generated the instant it's used, so the grid must always have at least as much supply as demand. If demand exceeds supply, the grid operator orders rolling blackouts ("load shed") to keep the whole system from collapsing.

Texas demand is U-shaped with temperature: it's lowest on mild days near 65 °F and climbs on both hot and cold days. Supply behaves differently. Heat shaves off a little capacity, but hard freezes can knock out a large share of it, because gas wells, pipelines, valves, and plant instruments freeze. That's a correlated shock: the same weather that pushes demand up also pushes supply down. In February 2021, Winter Storm Uri knocked out about 52 GW of generation, almost half the grid, right as demand was heading toward a winter record. The result was 20 GW of rolling blackouts, lasting days for some people.

What to notice as you move the sliders

Winterization only matters when it's cold

Set the temperature to 100 °F and drag winterization from 0% to 100%: nothing changes. Now set it to 10 °F and try again. The blue supply curve's "cliff" on the left gets shallower. The value of the investment depends entirely on the hazard. This is the hazard × vulnerability interaction.

Cold is worse than heat, even at similar demand

A 100 °F afternoon and a 15 °F morning produce similar demand. But on the hot afternoon, solar is producing and plants are running; on the frozen morning, it's dark and plants are failing. The red shaded gap shows up first on the cold side.

Growth squeezes both ends

Raise demand growth to 20% and watch the red gap open on both the hot and cold sides. Then use new capacity to close it. Notice how much capacity a heat wave needs compared with a freeze when winterization is low.

Where the numbers come from

This is a simplified teaching model, not ERCOT's operational model. Its parameters are set so that it reproduces real recorded events:

  • Summer peak: ERCOT's official all-time demand record is 85,508 MW on Aug. 10, 2023. The model gives ~85.6 GW at 101 °F. (ERCOT peak demand records; an unofficial grid-tracker reading of ~85.9 GW followed in Aug. 2024, KPRC.)
  • Winter peak: 78,314 MW on Jan. 16, 2024, in the 7–8 a.m. hour, with no emergency operations. The model gives ~77.6 GW at 18 °F with supply still ahead of demand. (ERCOT Monthly, Feb 2024)
  • Winter Storm Uri: ~52 GW (48.6%) of generation forced offline at the worst point, 20 GW peak load shed, and an estimated 76.8 GW demand without load shed. Most of the loss was gas-fired plants, many short of fuel. The model gives ~77 GW demand and ~56 GW supply at 6 °F with 0% winterization, a ~21 GW shortfall, close to the 20 GW actually shed. (The model starts from a smaller usable baseline than total installed capacity, so it needs fewer GW of outages to get there.) (S&P Global; POWER Magazine)
  • Solar at the peak hour: winter peaks happen in the dark early morning, so solar contributes almost nothing then; summer peaks are late afternoon. (ERCOT news release, Jan 15, 2024)
  • Homes per MW: ERCOT's rule of thumb is that 1 MW serves about 200 homes at peak. Treat the household number as a rough order of magnitude. (ERCOT via CBS DFW)

Model assumptions: demand = 55 GW at 65 °F, rising 0.85 GW per °F above that and 0.48 GW per °F below it, scaled by the growth slider. Supply = 78 GW of firm capacity + 7 GW of wind + up to 16 GW of solar (fading on colder days, since those peaks happen before sunrise) + new capacity, minus heat derating above 90 °F, minus cold-weather outages that start below 35 °F and reach 21 GW at 6 °F when nothing is winterized. Winterization removes up to 90% of the cold outages. Real grids also depend on wind conditions, transmission limits, plant maintenance, and how long the event lasts, none of which are modeled here.
